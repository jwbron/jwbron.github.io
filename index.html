  • Attention is all you need (to protect)

    Human attention is the scarcest resource in AI-assisted work. AI output can spend it or save it, and people can only choose where to spend it once they trust what the agent did.

    October 27, 2026
  • Give agents a contract, not a conversation

    When intent lives in a chat, agents drift. Write it down somewhere they can't rewrite.

    October 22, 2026
  • "Nothing to decide" is a claim

    Agents settle questions that were yours, because asking is more work than deciding. Make every decision visible, including the ones they chose not to bring you.

    November 15, 2026
  • Reseed, don't compact

    Long agent work shouldn't depend on a summarizer deciding what to forget.

    October 22, 2026
  • Agreement isn't verification

    When AI reviewers agree, that tells you their reasoning hangs together. It doesn't tell you the work is right.

    October 22, 2026
  • Teams, not swarms

    A group of agents in a shared chat is a swarm. A team needs a protocol.

    October 22, 2026
  • The model owns judgment, code owns the clock

    Models are good at judgment and unreliable at bookkeeping. Give each job to the thing that's good at it.

    October 22, 2026
  • Stop telling AI agents what not to do

    A prompt that says "don't" is a suggestion. Take the capability away instead.

    October 22, 2026
  • Connectors with enforceable boundaries

    Agents get useful when they reach your real systems. Design those connections so a confused or manipulated agent can't do damage through them.

What I work on

Guardrails and safe connectors

I give agents access to tools, integrations and data through connections they can't misuse. They only get the access a task needs, and a person approves the decisions that matter.

I've spent years working with sensitive student, health and financial data.

Evaluations and cost

Repeatable tests of how agents and models actually perform, with cost tracked per model. I break spend down by model, role and phase, so it's clear what each workflow costs.

Review agents that help

Agents that check work before anyone relies on it, whether that's a contract, a research memo, a report or code. Findings are checked against the source before they reach a person, follow-ups only look at what changed, and the people using it shape how it works. The AI code reviewer I built covered every pull request at about a fifth of the estimated cost of the managed tool it replaced.

Teaching and adoption

I teach teams, engineers and non-engineers alike, to work well with AI, set practical best practices, and keep the tools simple enough that they stick.

Faster, cheaper delivery

Build, test and deployment pipelines and cloud platforms that are quick, cheap to run and easy for a team to own. My self-hosted CI runners at Khan Academy cut compute costs in half.

How I work

  1. I start with how the team works today. Before changing anything, I learn the existing workflow from the people in it.
  2. Then I pick one bounded problem. A review workflow, an agent setup, or a slow and expensive system. Something with a clear before and after, and a way to measure it.
  3. I build it with the people who'll use it. Their feedback shapes it, and I own the rollout and the rough edges along with the first version.
  4. I make it something the team can run. Reusable infrastructure and clear ownership, so it keeps working without me.
